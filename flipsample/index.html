Rizal's Journey to Heroism

Experiences, ideas, and courageous choices shaped a young man from Calamba into a national hero. Follow the story one scene at a time.

In June 1861, a boy was born into a comfortable family in a Laguna town ruled by a distant empire. Nothing about his birth marked him for history. He studied, travelled, wrote, argued, healed, and finally accepted the consequences of his convictions.

Central question: How did an ordinary Filipino's experiences, education, writings, and choices contribute to his becoming a national hero? The answer is not given up front. Gather it chapter by chapter.

Chapter 1: The Young Rizal

A lakeside town, a household of readers, and a childhood lesson in how power could be misused.

José Protasio Rizal Mercado y Alonso Realonda was born in Calamba, a farming town on the shore of Laguna de Bay. He was the seventh of eleven children, and the only boy among the younger siblings. His father, Francisco Mercado, and his mother, Teodora Alonso, were hard-working tenants on land owned by a religious order. They were not poor, and they could afford books, tutors, and schooling, which was rare for a provincial Filipino family of the time.

Rizal's first teacher was his mother. She taught him letters and prayers, and encouraged him to love poetry and drawing. His older brother Paciano, who had studied in Manila, became his adviser and, later, a quiet supporter of his work. In 1870 he was sent to a teacher in nearby Biñan, where he found that learning could also mean memorizing and discipline. Much of his later impatience with rote schooling can be traced to such experiences, though he never wrote of them as a single turning point.

In 1871 his mother was arrested on a charge that her family and many later biographers considered false, and was taken to a jail in Santa Cruz, Laguna, far from home. Rizal was ten years old. The next year, three Filipino priests, Gómez, Burgos, and Zamora, were executed after the Cavite mutiny, and Paciano had been close to Burgos. The Rizal household learned, directly and painfully, that a respectable name did not protect anyone from arbitrary power.

None of this made Rizal a hero. A ten-year-old does not decide to be one. But it supplied questions that would stay with him: Who gets treated fairly? Who decides? What happens to people with no voice? His books, essays, and school in Dapitan can be read as long answers to those questions.

What happened?
In 1871 Rizal's mother was arrested and jailed, and in 1872 three Filipino priests were executed.
Why was it important?
It showed him, inside his own family, how colonial justice could be arbitrary and how little ordinary Filipinos could do about it.
How did it influence him?
Rizal later wrote about wrongful accusation, abuse of authority, and the fear that follows. Historians generally treat these events as one influence among many, not the sole cause.
What did he do as a result?
He dedicated his second novel to the three priests and kept returning to the theme of unjust trials.
How did it contribute to his journey to heroism?
It planted questions of justice that education, travel, and writing would later sharpen.

Key takeaway

Rizal's values grew from a learning-centered family and an early, personal encounter with injustice.

Chapter 2: The Learner

From a Calamba parlor to Manila, Madrid, Paris, Heidelberg, and Berlin: a mind built in many classrooms.

Learning before school

Rizal's education began at home. His mother taught him to read and pray, and tutors came to the house, among them Maestro Celestino, Maestro Lucas Padua, and later Leon Monroy, an old classmate of his father. Around 1869 he was sent to a private school in Biñan run by Justiniano Aquino Cruz. The lessons there were strict and heavy on memorization, and he later remembered the long hours and the punishments, but he also learned Latin and Spanish well enough to stand out.

Ateneo Municipal de Manila, 1872–1877

In 1872 Rizal entered the Ateneo Municipal, a Jesuit school in Intramuros. His Spanish was weaker than that of many classmates, so he took extra Spanish lessons outside school and worked until he reached the top of the class. The Jesuit method was demanding: daily recitation, competitions between students, and emphasis on character as well as grades. It also encouraged art, poetry, and science, and he found a mentor in Father Francisco de Paula Sánchez.

He graduated in March 1877 with a Bachelor of Arts and the highest marks. In parallel he completed the surveying course and passed the surveying examination, which later gave him a profession he could fall back on. At Ateneo he learned that discipline and curiosity could go together.

University of Santo Tomas, 1877–1882

Rizal then enrolled at the University of Santo Tomas, the oldest university in Manila, founded in 1611. He began with philosophy and letters, and in 1878 switched to medicine, a decision tied to his mother's failing eyesight. These were also years of writing. His poem to Filipino youth won recognition in 1879, and his allegorical play El Consejo de los Dioses was recognized in a literary contest in 1880.

Yet by his own accounts he was dissatisfied. He felt that the teaching was rigid, and that Filipino students were not treated as equals to their Spanish classmates. That feeling helped push him to decide, in 1882, to continue his studies in Spain, partly without telling his parents.

Madrid, 1882–1885

In Madrid, Rizal enrolled at the Universidad Central in two programs at once: medicine, and philosophy and letters. He completed the medical licentiate in 1884 and the philosophy and letters work in 1885. His learning was never confined to those lecture halls. He took painting and sculpture classes at the Academy of Fine Arts of San Fernando, studied languages, and trained in fencing and shooting at a gymnasium. He also read widely, joined Filipino circles in the city, and watched how Spaniards debated politics in public.

Paris and Heidelberg, 1885–1886

After finishing in Madrid, he left to specialize in eye diseases. In Paris he worked in the clinic of the ophthalmologist Louis de Wecker. In early 1886 he moved to Heidelberg, where he trained in the university's eye clinic under Otto Becker and also studied German. In the evenings and weekends he wrote letters, sketched, and composed poetry, including "A las flores de Heidelberg".

Berlin and self-education

In late 1886 he went to Berlin, where he joined scientific societies and met scholars such as Rudolf Virchow and Feodor Jagor, who respected his work on Philippine languages and ethnology. This was the most independent stage of his learning. No curriculum assigned him anything; he studied what he thought a Filipino writer needed to know. By common accounts he came to know more than ten languages, which let him read sources directly rather than through translators.

Chapter 3: The Traveler

Each port taught him something about power, freedom, and what a nation could be.

Rizal travelled for about a decade, and his journeys had a pattern. In Spain he met Filipino students and liberal Spaniards. In France and Germany he met scientists, ethnologists, and ordinary families who welcomed him. In Britain he spent long days in a famous library. In each place he compared what he saw with life in the Philippines.

Spain, 1882–85. He sailed past Singapore, Colombo, Suez, and Naples. In Madrid he met other Filipinos, including future Propaganda leaders, and saw that a Spanish liberal press could criticize its own government. For a young Filipino, that was a revelation.

Paris, Heidelberg, Berlin, 1885–87. He worked with eye specialists, stayed in a German pastor's home in Wilhelmsfeld, and joined scientific societies in Berlin. There, among scholars who treated him as an equal, he finished Noli Me Tangere, printed in 1887 with money lent by a friend, Maximo Viola.

Asia and America, 1888. After a short return to Calamba, where he saw his family's land dispute with the hacienda, he left again via Hong Kong and Japan, then crossed the United States by train. In Japan he admired the order and courtesy of society, and in America he noticed both its opportunities and its racial prejudice.

London, Paris, Brussels, Ghent, 1888–91. In London he read early Spanish accounts of the islands in the British Museum and prepared his edition of Morga's history. He then joined the reform campaign in Spain and Paris and finished El Filibusterismo in Ghent. He ended the decade in Hong Kong, setting up a clinic before returning to Manila in June 1892.

What happened?
Over about ten years he lived in or visited Spain, France, Germany, Britain, Belgium, Japan, Hong Kong, and the United States.
Why was it important?
It let him compare legal systems, schools, and colonial attitudes first-hand.
How did it influence him?
His essays and letters repeatedly measure Philippine conditions against what he had observed elsewhere, rather than treating the colony as the only possible world.
What did he do as a result?
He researched Philippine history in London and wrote for Filipino readers from abroad.
How did it contribute to his journey to heroism?
Travel widened him from personal grievance to a national outlook.

Key takeaway

Travel gave Rizal comparison, contacts, and the distance to describe his country clearly.

Chapter 4: The Writer

He chose the pen because it could reach people who would never meet him.

Why he wrote

Rizal began writing as a student. In 1879 he won a literary contest with a poem addressed to Filipino youth, and in 1880 his allegorical play El Consejo de los Dioses was recognized in another. In 1882 he published an essay on love of country under the pen name Laong Laan. These early works show a young man testing what writing could do. By the time he reached Europe, he had decided it could do more than entertain: it could describe a society to itself and to the people who ruled it.

Noli Me Tangere, 1887

Rizal proposed writing a novel about the Philippines while still in Madrid in 1884, and drafted parts of it in Madrid and Paris. He finished it in Berlin in early 1887. Money ran short, and the printing was made possible by a loan from his friend Máximo Viola. The first copies came out in March 1887.

The title comes from Latin words meaning "touch me not", and the book is a dedication to his country. It follows Crisóstomo Ibarra, a young man back from Europe who wants to build a school in his hometown of San Diego. He meets the arrogance of Padre Dámaso, the scheming of Padre Salví, the sorrow of the mother Sisa, and the idealism of the boatman Elías. Rizal packed the book with characters who stand for different parts of colonial society, from the powerful to the powerless.

Spanish authorities and religious orders reacted angrily, and the book was banned in the Philippines. Copies still entered the country, were passed among readers, and were treated by officials as a sign of suspicion. Whether readers loved or hated it, it made people talk about things that were usually left unsaid.

El Filibusterismo, 1891

Rizal began the sequel in London in 1888 and finished it in 1891. It is a darker, faster book. Its central figure, Simoun, a wealthy jeweler who is secretly Ibarra returned in disguise, plans a violent plot against the system. Around him are the student Isagani, the young Basilio, the farmer Kabesang Tales, and Padre Florentino. The plot fails, and the novel asks whether revenge can ever fix what injustice has broken.

Printing was again a struggle. In Ghent, Belgium, Rizal ran out of money, and a friend, Valentín Ventura, advanced funds so the printing could be completed. The novel appeared in September 1891, dedicated to the priests Gómez, Burgos, and Zamora, whose execution in 1872 had left a mark on his youth.

Essays, history, and letters

Rizal's essays were shorter and more direct. "The Philippines a Century Hence" (1889–90) considered what might happen to the colony if reforms were refused. "On the Indolence of the Filipino" (1890) answered the common claim that Filipinos were lazy by pointing to the heat, the forced labor, the lack of incentive, and the discouragement built into colonial life. Both appeared in La Solidaridad. In 1889 he also wrote a Tagalog letter to a group of young women in Malolos who had asked for a night school.

He also turned to history. In London he read the early Spanish chronicle Sucesos de las Islas Filipinas by Antonio de Morga, first printed in 1609, and published an annotated edition in Paris in 1890. His notes were meant to show that Filipinos had a society and culture before Spain arrived, and that the descriptions written by colonizers should not be taken as the whole truth.

His letters are another part of the record. He wrote to his family, to fellow reformers such as Marcelo H. del Pilar and Mariano Ponce, and to the Austrian scholar Ferdinand Blumentritt, with whom he exchanged letters from 1886 on. They show how his ideas developed over time and how he explained his plans to different audiences. Historians use them to check what he said in public against what he said in private.

His last writings came in exile and prison, including the poem "Mi último adiós," written in December 1896. Taken together, his works describe conditions, argue that Filipinos are capable of self-government, and model how to answer power with evidence.

More of his important writings

The two novels are only part of a large body of work. Rizal wrote poems, plays, essays, scholarly notes, translations, letters, and political documents over about twenty years. Each group below served a different purpose, and reading them side by side shows how his thinking grew from schoolboy verse to national argument.

Chapter 5: The Reformer

Documented goals first, later interpretations second.

By the late 1880s, Filipino students and professionals in Spain were campaigning for change in what became known as the Propaganda Movement. They asked for representation in the Spanish parliament, equal treatment under the law, freedom of speech and the press, and a larger role for Filipino priests. Their paper, La Solidaridad, was founded in Barcelona in February 1889, and Rizal wrote for it under pen names such as Laong Laan and Dimasalang.

Rizal's main emphasis was education and moral responsibility. He wrote that rights were not simply given; people had to show their worth. He admired Marcelo H. del Pilar and Graciano López Jaena, the paper's leaders, but he disagreed with them at times about tactics and leadership, a reminder that reform movements were made of real people with real differences.

In 1891–92 he explored resettling Calamba tenants in British North Borneo, an idea that went nowhere. He returned to Manila and, on July 3, 1892, founded the Liga Filipina, a civic association meant to unite Filipinos, protect members, support education, agriculture, and commerce, and encourage reform. Three days later he was arrested and soon deported to Dapitan. Andrés Bonifacio founded the Katipunan the day after Rizal's arrest, and the Katipunan later used Rizal's name, but Rizal himself did not join it.

This is why historians separate what Rizal did from what others made of him. He sought reform and criticized the premature 1896 uprising. Yet his writings and his example inspired those who chose revolution. Both facts are true, and a careful reader keeps them apart.

Chapter 6: The Exile

Banished to a small town, he built.

From July 1892 to July 1896, Rizal lived in Dapitan, a small coastal town in Mindanao now part of Zamboanga del Norte. He was not imprisoned in the usual sense. He could walk, work, and receive visitors, though he was watched and could not leave. The local commandant, Ricardo Carnicero, became a friend. When Rizal won a share of a lottery prize, he used it to buy land in Talisay, built a house, and set up a farm.

He opened a clinic and treated patients, including eye cases that others had given up on. His mother travelled to Dapitan in 1895, and he operated on her eyes. He started a school for boys, who learned languages, mathematics, sciences, and practical trades, and he made a relief map of Mindanao in the town plaza. He helped improve the town's water supply, introduced better farming and trade, and studied local plants, animals, and languages.

Exile restricted his freedom but not his usefulness. In Dapitan the education he had gained in Manila and Europe became practical: medical care, teaching, small engineering, and farming. The same values he had written about, such as dignity, learning, and responsibility to others, appeared in daily acts. He also continued corresponding with scholars around the world, so isolation never became intellectual silence.

Chapter 7: The Final Journey

A sober account of his last months.

In mid-1896 the Katipunan was preparing a revolt. One of its members, Pío Valenzuela, visited Rizal in Dapitan to seek his view; Rizal advised against starting an uprising without proper preparation. Soon afterward, he asked to serve as a doctor in Cuba, and the governor-general agreed. He left Dapitan on July 31, 1896. While he was at sea, the revolt broke out in August.

He was arrested on the way to Spain and sent back to Manila, where he was imprisoned in Fort Santiago. On December 15 he wrote a manifesto condemning the uprising and denying involvement. He was nevertheless charged with rebellion and tried by a military court on December 26. The verdict was death.

On the morning of December 30, 1896, Rizal was taken to Bagumbayan, the field that is now part of Rizal Park, and shot by a firing squad. He was thirty-five. The night before, he wrote his farewell poem, and accounts describe him as calm. What he did during those last hours matters less for drama than for what it shows: he did not retract his belief in reform, and he did not ask others to take up arms in his name.

His death shocked many Filipinos, including those who had never read his books. It turned a respected writer into a martyr almost overnight. Colonial authorities believed that his execution would end a threat; for many Filipinos, it had the opposite effect.

Chapter 8: The Hero

Putting the evidence together.

A hero is shaped by what a person does and by how others remember it. Rizal's life follows a sequence. A learning-centered family gave him values. An early encounter with injustice gave him questions. Schooling gave him tools. Travel gave him comparison. Writing gave him voice. Reform gave him purpose. Dapitan gave him proof of sincerity. His death gave him a place in the nation's memory.

How the stages connect

Values and curiosity. His mother's lessons and his love of learning appear in everything he later did, from his Ateneo years to his school in Dapitan. Exposure to ideas. In Europe, he saw how a free press and open universities worked, and that experience strengthened his conviction that Filipinos deserved more. Writing. He decided to use books and essays to describe social problems, knowing the risks.

Reform. He argued for education, equality, and representation, and formed the Liga Filipina. Exile. In Dapitan he showed that his principles could be lived locally. Death. His execution made him a symbol, and the way he faced it shaped how later generations judged him.

Documented intentions and later meaning

What Rizal documented was a desire for reform, education, and dignity for Filipinos, and a rejection of the 1896 uprising as premature. What others later attributed to him was more: father of the nation, symbol of revolution, even a figure for national identity. In 1956, Republic Act No. 1425, the Rizal Law, required students to study his life and writings, which made him a shared reference point, though not without debate. A critical reader respects both his own record and the meaning that grew around it.

What happened?
Across thirty-five years, experiences moved him from student to writer to reformer to exile.
Why was it important?
Each stage supplied something the next one required: values, skills, perspective, voice, and credibility.
How did it influence him?
His writings show a steady concern with justice, education, and responsibility.
What did he do as a result?
He chose open argument and service over silence, even when it was costly.
How did it contribute to his journey to heroism?
No single event explains it. The combination of ideas, choices, and consequences, interpreted by later Filipinos, did.

Reflection for Filipino youth

Rizal's life suggests habits worth practicing: study seriously, think critically, communicate responsibly, take part in civic life, and act with courage for others. We should not assume he would endorse every modern opinion or movement. The lesson is the method, not a script.

  • What problem in your community deserves your attention?
  • How can you check a claim before sharing it online?
  • What small act of service could you begin this month?

Key takeaway

Rizal became a hero through connected experiences, deliberate choices, and the meaning later generations gave them.

Reflect: What would you want your own choices to say about you?
